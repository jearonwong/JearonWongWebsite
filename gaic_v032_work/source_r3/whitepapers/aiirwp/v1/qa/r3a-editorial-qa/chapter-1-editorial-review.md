# Chapter 1 Editorial Review

**Chapter:** The New Insurance Question: What Exactly Is Being Transferred?
**Decision:** keep

## Findings

Chapter 1 is the strongest R3A chapter editorially. It opens from a recognizable meeting: business leader, broker, CFO, CTO, counsel, and board all asking for a direct answer to whether AI agents are insured. That scenario creates immediate reader pain and avoids abstract taxonomy.

The refund/account/API scenario is concrete enough to do real work. It shows how a customer-support agent can approve refunds, update records, trigger external APIs, send notices, and create downstream harm. The question stack that follows is useful: insured subject, policy line, work object, delegated authority, human role, dependency, event trigger, exclusion/limitation/sublimit review, evidence, and remediation.

The chapter clearly establishes that the company, human actor, officer, professional, vendor, or platform may matter under different lines or legal relationships, while the AI agent is usually not the insured legal subject. It then introduces the bounded work unit without overclaiming that it is an external insurance standard.

The table is useful. It compresses the insurance transfer question stack into a format that can survive later artifact work. It does not feel like filler.

The bridge to Chapter 2 works. The chapter ends by saying that the market is not answering with one yes/no; it is covering, excluding, bounding, limiting, leaving silent, and packaging narrower model-performance risks.

## Notes

- Keep the refund/account/API scenario. It is the best evidence that R3A is reader-facing rather than theory-first.
- In a later full-body edit, consider making the first principle sentence visually prominent: "The insured subject and the agentic risk object must be separated."
- Do not add more source material to this chapter unless it sharpens the scenario; the chapter already has enough support.
