# Chapter 3 Editorial Review

**Chapter:** Why LLM Insurance Is Not Agentic Lifecycle Insurance
**Decision:** keep

## Findings

Chapter 3 clearly separates "AI insurance" labels from lifecycle risk transfer. It makes the key distinction without unfairly attacking market products: model-performance products, AI warranties, AI cyber coverages, and professional liability contexts can all be useful, but they ask different questions.

The workflow example is strong. A vendor model produces a plausible output, the agent selects an action, a tool updates a customer account, a human reviewer approves a summary, and loss follows. The example shows why model performance can be acceptable while the agentic work still fails through context, authority, tool choice, or weak review.

The chapter's question sequence works:

- model-performance insurance asks about defined performance promises;
- cyber coverage asks about cyber-defined events such as unauthorized access or compute abuse;
- professional liability asks about professional service or advice failure;
- agentic lifecycle risk asks how the work was authorized, planned, executed, reviewed, accepted, evidenced, changed, and remediated.

The treatment of logs and traces is balanced. The chapter says traces and logs are useful and often essential, but insufficient by themselves as claim evidence. That is the right tone.

The WP2 bridge is useful and bounded. Audit Evidence Chain logic is presented as a reconstruction aid, not a claim outcome.

The bridge to Part II is clean. The final question about company, human, officer, professional, vendor, AI agent, or bounded work unit sets up the core object problem without prematurely introducing AIO/AIRM.
