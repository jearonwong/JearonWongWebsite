## Recommended Title

AI Agents Are Not the Insured Subject

- The Company May Be Insured. The AI Agent Usually Is Not.

## Draft

AI agents are not the insured legal subject. They are agentic risk objects whose actions must be mapped back to human roles, corporate responsibility, coverage boundaries, and reconstructable lifecycle evidence.

That distinction sounds technical, but it is where the insurance conversation starts to become real.

When a company deploys an AI agent, the policy usually does not insure the agent as if it were a person, officer, contractor, or separately accountable legal entity. The company may be insured. A professional may be insured. A director, employee, vendor, or business process may sit inside a coverage analysis. But the agent itself is usually a system component whose actions have to be traced back to a responsible legal subject.

That creates a problem for risk transfer.

If an AI agent books the wrong transaction, sends the wrong instruction, misuses a tool, exposes data, produces a defective recommendation, or triggers an operational loss, the core question is not simply "what did the model output?"

The harder questions are:

- Who had authority over the work?
- Which human role owned the task?
- What business process did the agent act within?
- Which tool permissions mattered?
- Which confirmation, escalation, or override paths existed?
- What evidence reconstructs the loss event?
- Where does the coverage boundary begin and end?

Logs are useful, but logs are not the same thing as claim evidence. A log may show that something happened. It may not show who had authority, what duty applied, which policy boundary is implicated, whether the action was approved, whether the loss was foreseeable, or whether the event fits a covered risk category.

Human-in-the-loop language has a similar problem. Saying that a human was "in the loop" does not by itself map responsibility. Was the human supervising, approving, reviewing, rubber-stamping, delegating, or merely available? Which work unit did they own? What decision rights did they hold? What evidence would let a claims reviewer reconstruct that role after the fact?

GAIC establishes the lifecycle governance foundation. AIAAWP interprets that foundation through auditability and assurance. AIIRWP applies the same lifecycle logic to insurability, claims reconstruction, and risk transfer.

The paper introduces two analytical concepts:

Agentic Insurability Objects, or AIOs, describe the objects insurers, brokers, risk teams, auditors, and enterprise leaders need to distinguish when asking whether an AI-enabled loss can be understood. They include the insured legal subject, the agentic risk object, the human role, the work unit, the tool permission, the business authority, the loss event, the evidence chain, the dependency layer, and other insurability-relevant objects.

The Agentic Insurability Readiness Model, or AIRM, describes maturity levels for how well an enterprise can reconstruct agentic AI activity for risk-transfer analysis. It is not a standard, certification, or underwriting rule. It is a readiness vocabulary for asking whether the organization has enough lifecycle evidence to support serious insurance discussion.

The central argument is simple:

Agentic AI will not become meaningfully insurable just because systems produce more logs. It becomes more legible to risk transfer when agent actions can be mapped to legal subjects, human roles, business authority, work units, loss events, coverage boundaries, and evidence chains.
