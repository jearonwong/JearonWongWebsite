AI agents are not the insured subject.

That sentence is the beginning of the AI insurance problem.

A company can be insured. A person can hold authority. A contract can allocate responsibility. A policy can define coverage boundaries. But the agentic system is usually not the legal subject that the policy is written around.

So the real question is not "can we insure the AI agent?"

The better question is:

Can the enterprise reconstruct what the agent did, why it acted, who authorized the operating context, what controls shaped the decision path, and how the event maps back to human roles, corporate responsibility, and coverage language?

Logs are not claim evidence by default.

Framework traces are useful, but insufficient by themselves.

Audit trails help, but risk transfer needs more than observability. It needs a lifecycle evidence chain: role authority, configuration, runtime context, tool use, handoffs, exceptions, human review, incident closure, and boundary language that can survive scrutiny after loss.

That is the thesis of the third paper in the Agentic Lifecycle Governance Industry Series:

*Agentic AI Insurability & Risk Transfer White Paper 2026*

It builds on WP1, *Global AI Compliance White Paper 2026*, and WP2, *Agentic AI Auditability & Assurance White Paper 2026*, by asking what evidence would matter to insurers, reinsurers, brokers, CROs, CFOs, CTOs, counsel, boards, and claims teams.

The paper introduces two bounded analytical vocabularies:

- AIO: Agentic Insurability Object
- AIRM: Agentic Insurability Readiness Model

These are not certifications. They are not insurer acceptance. They are not coverage-ready labels. They are a way to reason about evidence readiness without pretending that auditability automatically equals insurability.
